🏛️ The Real Empire State Building: A Field Guide

Born out of a Roaring Twenties ego contest between General Motors executive John Jakob Raskob and Walter Chrysler over who could build the tallest skyscraper in Manhattan, the Empire State Building went up at a pace that would give a modern OSHA inspector a heart attack. Starting on St. Patrick's Day in 1930, three thousand four hundred ironworkers (including hundreds of Mohawk skywalkers from Kahnawake) riveted fifty-seven thousand tons of steel into place at a rate of four and a half stories per week, finishing the entire hundred-and-two-story tower in just one year and forty-five days. Its famous two-hundred-foot pinnacle was originally designed as a mooring mast for transatlantic zeppelins, an idea quietly abandoned after a single 1931 test where sixty-mile-per-hour updrafts nearly flipped a Navy blimp over Thirty-Fourth Street.

When the building opened in May 1931 in the depths of the Great Depression, so many offices sat unrented that New Yorkers mocked it as the "Empty State Building," and the building only stayed solvent thanks to tourists paying a dollar to ride up to the observation deck. Today, that ticket costs between forty-four and seventy-nine dollars, depending on whether you want the open-air eighty-sixth-floor deck or the glass tube on the hundred-and-second floor. Before you ever smell the Midtown breeze, however, you must wind through forty-five minutes of velvet-rope velvet mazes, mandatory green-screen photo booths, and an immersive museum featuring giant animatronic King Kong fingers poking through faux office walls.

Yet @BadCallBobby rode those high-speed Otis elevators expecting a Major League Baseball rules committee, leaving a furious review titled "You're OUT-ragous!" because the "Umpire State Building" was "just one giant, yelling sports official" where every call was questioned. Confusing a twelve-hundred-and-fifty-foot Art Deco masterpiece on Fifth Avenue with a grumpy man in a chest protector behind home plate is a swing and a miss of historic proportions. If you start screaming at the uniformed security guards on the eighty-sixth-floor promenade about a blown strike-three call at Yankee Stadium, they will happily eject you back down to Thirty-Fourth Street.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Buy a sunset-slot ticket several days ahead online, ignore the uniformed street hawkers outside on Fifth Avenue selling fake 'express' passes, and bring a windbreaker even in July.
