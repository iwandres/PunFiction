🏛️ The Real Hollywood Sign: A Field Guide

When Los Angeles newspaper publisher Harry Chandler erected a giant billboard on the southern slope of Mount Lee in 1923, he was not celebrating cinema; he was trying to sell segregated suburban housing lots in a subdivision called "Hollywoodland." The original sign cost twenty-one thousand dollars, spelled out all thirteen letters in whitewashed wood and sheet metal, and flashed at night with four thousand twenty-watt lightbulbs tended by a lone maintenance man living in a shack behind one of the L's. Intended to last only eighteen months, the sign rotted undisturbed until 1949, when the wooden H blew over and the Chamber of Commerce agreed to repair it on the condition that "LAND" be removed. By 1978, termites had eaten so much of the remaining frame that Hugh Hefner hosted a charity gala to fund the current forty-five-foot steel letters.

Attempting to touch those steel letters today is one of the fastest ways to meet the Los Angeles Police Department. Surrounded by motion sensors, infrared cameras, and loud speakers that bark pre-recorded warnings at trespassers, the immediate perimeter is strictly off-limits, largely because wealthy Beachwood Canyon residents grew tired of lost tourists blocking their driveways for selfies. Visitors must hike up the dusty, shade-free asphalt of Mount Hollywood Drive or the Brush Canyon Trail, dodging unleashed labradoodles and fitness influencers filming glute workouts in ninety-degree smog. From the legal viewing platform above the ridge, you mostly see the back of the corrugated metal framework and a vast, hazy expanse of the San Fernando Valley.

That long uphill slog clearly soured @BlazeItOrHateIt, whose review, "Total Buzzkill!", complained that "this nine-letter landmark definitely needs more green to live up to its name" after failing to locate any actual botanical buds. In fairness to the reviewer, pranksters led by Danny Finegood famously draped black-and-white curtains over the two O's on New Year's Day in 1976 to read "Hollyweed" in celebration of relaxed California marijuana laws. Still, hiking three miles up a chaparral fire road expecting the City of Los Angeles to run a municipal cannabis farm inside a sheet-metal consonant is a special kind of optimism.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Skip the residential streets with fake 'No Access' signs and hike up from the Griffith Observatory parking lot or take the Dash bus to the Canyon Drive trailhead.
