🏛️ The Real Everglades National Park: A Field Guide

For most of the nineteenth century, the official attitude of the Florida state legislature toward the Everglades was that it had far too much water in it and ought to be turned into celery farms immediately. In 1905, Governor Napoleon Bonaparte Broward campaigned on a promise to drain the entire swamp, digging hundreds of miles of canals before hydrologists gently pointed out that removing the fresh water would ruin South Florida's drinking supply. What survived this engineering enthusiasm was finally protected as a national park in 1947, largely thanks to environmentalist Marjory Stoneman Douglas. She accurately pointed out that the region is not actually a stagnant swamp at all, but a fifty-mile-wide, six-inch-deep limestone river moving southward toward Florida Bay at the leisurely pace of roughly a third of a mile per day.

Visiting this subtropical wilderness today requires an unhealthy tolerance for ninety percent humidity and insects that treat DEET like a light vinaigrette. Tourists gather along the Anhinga Trail boardwalk or cram onto ear-splitting propeller airboats outside the park boundaries, wearing plastic earmuffs while guides point at floating logs that occasionally blink. Between June and November, the mosquito population becomes dense enough to register on weather radar, and purple gallinules brazenly patrol the parking lots looking for dropped granola bars. Worse still, native American alligators now share the brackish canals with invasive Burmese pythons and the occasional stray crocodile, creating a wetland lounge where humans are decisively toward the bottom of the food chain.

Into this steaming ecosystem waded @GlazeGuzzler, who left a scathing review titled "A Glaze-ing Disappointment!" after discovering that the "sticky sweet glaze" tasted "suspiciously like Florida swamp water." One has to admire the sheer gastro-optimism of a traveler who drives an hour south of Miami, pays a thirty-five-dollar vehicle entrance fee at the Ernest F. Coe Visitor Center, and kneels beside a tannin-stained slough expecting Krispy Kreme syrup. Mistaking a one-point-five-million-acre wetland preserve for an artisanal bakery takes real commitment, especially when the local pastry chefs have eighty conical teeth and weigh four hundred pounds.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Keep at least fifteen feet away from the local wildlife, and never sample the brown water near the Shark Valley observation tower, no matter how much it resembles maple frosting.
