🏛️ The Real Stonehenge: A Field Guide

Constructed in stages between 3000 BCE and 1500 BCE by Neolithic Britons who lacked both the wheel and metal tools, Stonehenge is a masterclass in prehistoric stubbornness. While the outer twenty-five-ton sarsen sandstone blocks were dragged on timber sledges from the Marlborough Downs twenty miles away, eighty smaller bluestones were somehow hauled a hundred and forty miles from the Preseli Hills in western Wales. What few tourists realize is that the monument was heavily tidied up in the twentieth century: after philanthropist Cecil Chubb bought the circle at a 1915 auction on a whim for sixty-six hundred pounds and gave it to the nation, engineers spent the 1950s using mobile cranes and concrete foundations to straighten leaning pillars and re-seat fallen lintels.

Visiting the Salisbury Plain today begins a mile and a half west of the stones at a sleek visitor center, where crowds line up in a damp Wiltshire crosswind for diesel shuttle buses. Unless you pay extra for a special dawn access tour, a low rope barrier keeps you thirty yards away from the actual circle, ensuring that your experience consists of walking a slow loop on a paved path while listening to an audio guide describe solstice alignments. Just a couple of hundred yards away, a perpetual queue of heavy lorries and campervans crawls along the single-lane A303 highway, providing a picturesque backdrop of brake lights to five thousand years of Neolithic mystery.

That rope barrier was the least of the problems for @WobblyJoint, who filed "This place is utterly unhinged!" to complain that the giant stones "don't even open" and offer "zero entrance/exit functionality." Driving two hours west of London to inspect "Stone Hinge" as if it were a colossal Bronze Age hardware showroom is a triumph of literal-minded disappointment. It is true that the horizontal lintels are locked onto the upright pillars using woodworker-style mortise-and-tenon joints carved directly into the rock, which is impressive carpentry for 2500 BCE, even if none of the thirty-ton trilithons pivot open when you push on them.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Skip the crowded shuttle bus and walk the mile and a half from the visitor center across the open National Trust chalk downland through the prehistoric Cursus barrow cemetery.
