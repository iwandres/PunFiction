🏛️ The Real Roman Baths: A Field Guide

Around 60 CE, shortly after invading foggy Britannia and realizing their toes were permanently numb, Roman engineers marched into a Somerset valley where over a million liters of geothermal water bubbled out of the limestone every day at a steady forty-six degrees Celsius. Dedicated to the hybrid Celtic-Roman goddess Sulis Minerva, the town of Aquae Sulis grew around a massive lead-lined Great Bath fed by a complex subterranean plumbing system and vaulted with a forty-five-foot concrete barrel roof. While soaking, aggrieved Roman citizens scratched 130 vindictive curses onto thin sheets of lead and pewter and tossed them into the Sacred Spring, begging the goddess to strike blind or sleepless whichever lowlife had stolen their tunic, bath shoes, or six silver coins from the changing room.

The Roman roof caved in during the sixth century, burying the pools in black mud until Victorian architects built an ornate classical terrace with statues of Roman emperors around the upper rim in 1897. Today, you tour the subterranean museum with an audio guide voiced in part by Bill Bryson, peering down at the steaming, pea-green water of the Great Bath, which gets its alarming color from photosynthetic algae that thrive under the daylight. Because the ancient unlined spring conduits still harbor the occasional brain-eating amoeba, touching the water is strictly prohibited, though at the end of the tour you can pay a pound at the Pump Room fountain to drink a warm paper cup of clean filtered spa water that tastes like liquid bicycle spokes.

That sulfurous mineral bouquet clearly confused @NoodleNonsense, who filed a disgusted one-star review headlined 'Seriously Misleading Soak' after showing up in Somerset expecting to climb into a gigantic communal bowl of Japanese noodle soup. One has to admire the sheer determination of someone who takes the Great Western Railway from Paddington, walks past Bath Abbey in a bathrobe, and complains that the two-thousand-year-old Sacred Spring looks like 'lukewarm broth and suspicious toppings.' Furthermore, if your skin currently feels like you have been 'steeping in a giant soup bowl,' it means you ignored twelve separate warning signs and jumped over the Victorian balustrade into the algae, which explains why the security guards are running toward you.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Do not dip a finger into the green Roman pool; if you actually want to soak in the hot mineral water, book a rooftop session two blocks away at the modern Thermae Bath Spa.
