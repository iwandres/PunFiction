🏛️ The Real Dublin Castle: A Field Guide

For a building with "Castle" in the name, Dublin Castle looks suspiciously like a Georgian government ministry, mostly because nearly all of the medieval fortress burned down in a catastrophic fire in April 1684. King John of England ordered a stone castle built on the site of a Viking settlement in 1204 to guard his Norman treasure and watch the rebellious Irish, using water from the River Poddle to fill the moat (the dark pool where the Poddle met the Liffey, Dubh Linn, gave the city its name). Only the thirteenth-century Record Tower survives above ground from the medieval stronghold; the rest was rebuilt in the eighteenth century as brick courtyards and gilded state apartments for the British Viceroy until Michael Collins took over in January 1922.

Because Dublin Castle still functions as an active Irish government complex for presidential inaugurations and European Union summits, visiting can feel like touring a royal palace that occasionally has to go to the office. You stroll into the Upper Yard past uniformed Garda officers, buy an entry ticket for the grand State Apartments, and walk beneath Waterford crystal chandeliers and ceilings painted with allegories of King George III before descending into the subterranean Viking excavation. Down in the damp basement under the medieval Powder Tower, metal catwalks lead over the exposed stone foundations of the castle moat and tenth-century Norse embankments, smelling faintly of ancient river silt and modern dehumidifiers.

This centuries-long seat of colonial and republican administration completely baffled InnTrouble, who rolled a suitcase through the gates expecting the "Double-Inn Castle." In a furious hospitality critique titled "Inn-sufferable Stay!", the reviewer complains of "double the chaotic check-ins," fumes that they "paid for one stay, got two cramped 'inns' instead," and calls the complex "two poorly managed inn experiences." To wheel your carry-on bag into the State Apartments off Dame Street, demand a room key and a complimentary continental breakfast from an Irish heritage guide, and complain that a thirteenth-century royal fortress has poor front-desk service is peak travel confusion.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Pay the few extra euros for the guided tour rather than the self-guided ticket, as it is the only way to get down into the underground Viking moat excavations and the Chapel Royal.
