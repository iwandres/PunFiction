🏛️ The Real Mount Fuji: A Field Guide

Japan's highest peak is actually three volcanoes stacked on top of one another like geological nesting dolls, capped by the symmetrical cone of Shin-Fuji, which has been piling up basaltic lava and scoria for the last ten thousand years. Its most recent temper tantrum occurred in December 1707, when the Hoei eruption blasted eighty million cubic meters of volcanic ash across Edo (modern Tokyo) sixty miles away, darkening the midday sky and burying villages ten feet deep in black cinders. Revered for centuries by Shinto and Buddhist ascetics as a sacred dwelling of fire deities, Mount Fuji barred women from its upper slopes until 1868, when Lady Fanny Parkes, the wife of the British ambassador, marched to the summit in ankle-length Victorian skirts.

For ten months of the year, Mount Fuji is a snowy, wind-blasted ice cone best admired from the window of a Shinkansen bullet train, assuming the notorious summer cloud bank hasn't hidden the entire mountain behind grey fog. During the official July-to-September climbing season, however, four hundred thousand hikers converge on the Yoshida Trail, paying a mandatory two-thousand-yen toll at the fifth station before shuffling upward through loose, knee-deep volcanic gravel. By two in the morning, the upper switchbacks turn into a nose-to-tail conga line of glowing headlamps inching toward the crater rim in sub-zero gusts, while climbers pay a thousand yen to use bio-toilets and buy canned oxygen at mountainside huts.

None of this volcanic grit deterred @SugarShocked, whose review, "An Edible Catastrophe!", expressed outrage at finding no chocolatey dessert atop "Mound Fudgey." If you climb to twelve thousand three hundred and eighty-eight feet expecting a three-hundred-year-old stratovolcano to be a towering heap of warm chocolate fudge, the ensuing disappointment is entirely on you. In summer, when the snowcap melts completely away, the bare upper slopes of reddish-brown and black volcanic scoria really do look a bit like a stale brownie from a distance, though biting into a chunk of porous Pleistocene basalt will ruin your dental work much faster than sugar ever could.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: If you want to actually see the snow-capped peak clearly, visit the Five Lakes region on a crisp morning in December or January rather than during the cloudy summer climbing season.
