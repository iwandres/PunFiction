🏛️ The Real Brooklyn Bridge: A Field Guide

When the Brooklyn Bridge opened on May 24, 1883, its sixteen-hundred-foot main span was the longest suspension bridge on earth and the first to use steel wire instead of iron. The project nearly destroyed the Roebling family: chief designer John Augustus Roebling died of tetanus in 1869 after a ferry crushed his toes during surveying, and his son Washington Roebling suffered debilitating decompression sickness (then called caissons disease) after spending hours inside pressurized underwater wooden boxes at the river bottom. Confined to his Brooklyn Heights bedroom, Washington supervised the rest of the fourteen-year construction through a telescope, relying on his wife, Emily Warren Roebling, who mastered higher mathematics and catenary curve engineering to manage the contractors and become the first person to cross the finished bridge.

Crossing Emily and Washington's triumph today is less a romantic nineteenth-century promenade and more an obstacle course in high-altitude urban survival. Although the city finally banished cyclists to a separate lane on the lower roadway in 2021, the elevated wooden pedestrian boardwalk remains packed shoulder-to-shoulder with tourists stopping abruptly in the middle of traffic to pose beneath the pointed Gothic limestone arches. Between the rumble of six lanes of commuter cars humming beneath the floorboards, street vendors hawking plastic Statue of Liberty crowns, and wedding photographers commanding twenty-foot exclusion zones in the wind, getting the mile from Manhattan's City Hall to DUMBO takes forty-five minutes and saintly patience.

For @GreenerPastures, however, the real problem was culinary, as detailed in their review "A total cruciferous nightmare!", which griped that the cables were "just fibrous stems" forming a "giant, soggy vegetable span." It takes a rare dietary fixation to walk onto a landmark supported by four fifteen-inch-thick galvanized steel cables and mistake them for overcooked stalks of broccoli. Funnily enough, a crooked wire contractor named J. Lloyd Haigh actually did smuggle defective, brittle steel wire into the bridge's spinning wheels in 1878 before getting caught, though even Haigh never tried substituting fresh produce for high-tensile carbon steel.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Walk from the Brooklyn side toward Manhattan at sunrise so the skyline is directly in front of you and the midday crowds are still asleep in their hotels.
