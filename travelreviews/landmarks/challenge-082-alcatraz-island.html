🏛️ The Real Alcatraz Island: A Field Guide

Long before Al Capone played banjo in the prison band, the twenty-two-acre sandstone island in San Francisco Bay was charted in 1775 by Spanish explorer Juan Manuel de Ayala as La Isla de los Alcatraces ("Island of the Pelicans"). The Army turned it into a harbor fortress in the 1850s and a military prison during the Civil War before the Justice Department took over in 1934 to house federal inmates who kept escaping elsewhere. Cleverly, the cellhouse showers were plumbed with warm water so any convict who sawed through the bars would be too de-acclimatized to survive the fifty-two-degree bay currents. What finally closed the prison in 1963 was not Frank Morris digging out with spoons, but the astronomical cost of barging every gallon of fresh water to the rock.

While hardened bank robbers spent twenty-nine years trying to get off the island, modern tourists have to book tickets weeks in advance with Alcatraz City Cruises at Pier 33 just to get on it. After a fifteen-minute ferry ride through freezing bay fog, you hike up a switchback concrete road past nesting western gulls that smell aggressively of digested anchovies and scream at anyone who steps near their chicks. Inside the main cellblock, three hundred visitors at a time put on headsets and shuffle silently down "Broadway" in eerie synchronization, staring into five-by-nine-foot cells while the voices of actual former guards and inmates narrate the bloody 1946 "Battle of Alcatraz" in their ears.

This grim monument to federal penology proved a bitter letdown for WhiskeyWarden, who took the ferry across the bay expecting "Alcohol-traz Island." In a parched review titled "Sentenced to Sobriety!", our thirsty convict complains that "the only 'spirits' here are ghosts" and notes that there are "no real bars or mixers... just cold steel and seagulls." While "The Rock" does sound like an artisanal speakeasy in the Mission District where a bearded mixologist serves a twenty-four-dollar smoked old fashioned inside a birdcage, showing up at a maximum-security cellblock expecting happy hour and complaining about the wrong kind of bars is a felony against reading comprehension.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Book the evening night tour months in advance; watching the sun set behind the Golden Gate Bridge from the prison hospital windows while the cellhouse doors slam shut is well worth the extra jacket layer.
