🏛️ The Real Louvre Museum: A Field Guide

Before it held thirty-eight thousand works of art, the Louvre was a grim twelfth-century stone fortress built by King Philip II in 1190 to stop Viking and English raiders from sailing up the Seine while he was away on the Third Crusade. You can still walk down into the basement today and see the original medieval moat piers that Renaissance kings later buried beneath sprawling royal apartments. When Louis XIV grew tired of rebellious Parisian mobs in 1682 and moved his entire court out to Versailles, the abandoned palace briefly became a chaotic squatters' village for artists and stove-piped workshops until the French Revolutionaries nationalized the royal art collection and opened the building as a public museum in August 1793.

Entering that collection today starts in the Cour Napoléon, where tourists spend forty minutes taking forced-perspective photos pinching the tip of I. M. Pei's 1989 glass pyramid before descending into an underground shopping mall aroma of espresso and damp raincoats. With over four hundred rooms spread across three enormous wings, the Louvre is less an afternoon stroll and more a forced march through European history. Ninety percent of visitors sprint directly past five thousand years of Mesopotamian antiquities and Delacroix canvases to pack ten-deep into the Salle des États, where they hold phones above their heads to snap a blurry three-inch photo of the Mona Lisa behind two panes of bulletproof glass.

That artistic marathon left @ScrubbieDoo with an unusual dermatological complaint in "Left feeling completely unsmoothed.", grieving that the "Loofah Museum" was full of "scratchy fibers" and left their skin "scrubbed raw." Showing up on the Right Bank of Paris in a bathrobe expecting the world's largest art museum to be a showroom for fibrous shower sponges is a monumental failure of French pronunciation. We can only hope @ScrubbieDoo did not attempt to exfoliate their shoulders against the Winged Victory of Samothrace or Jacques-Louis David's twenty-by-thirty-foot canvas of the Coronation of Napoleon before security escorted them to the Rue de Rivoli.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Skip the long outdoor line at the central glass pyramid and enter through the underground Carrousel du Louvre shopping gallery on Rue de Rivoli with a pre-booked timed ticket.
