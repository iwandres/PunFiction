🏛️ The Real Yellowstone National Park: A Field Guide

When mountain man John Colter walked out of the Wyoming wilderness in 1808 and described spouting fountains of boiling water and bubbling pits of sulfurous mud, East Coast newspapers laughed it off as "Colter's Hell" and assumed he was delirious. Not until the 1871 Hayden Geological Survey brought back photographs and paintings did Congress believe the stories, prompting President Ulysses S. Grant to sign legislation in 1872 creating Yellowstone as the world's first national park. What sits beneath the park's pine forests is actually an active supervolcano caldera thirty miles by forty-five miles wide, where a shallow underground magma chamber superheats groundwater to power more than ten thousand hydrothermal vents and half the planet's active geysers.

Navigating this geothermal pressure cooker in July is an exercise in bumper-to-bumper patience. Whenever a solitary bison decides to take an afternoon stroll down the middle of the Grand Loop Road, traffic grinds to a halt for five miles in both directions in what rangers affectionately call a "bison jam." Around the wooden boardwalks of the Upper Geyser Basin and Grand Prismatic Spring, gusts of wind blow clouds of rotten-egg hydrogen sulfide steam directly into tourists' faces while blowing their baseball caps onto the fragile orange bacterial mats. Every summer, at least one visitor ignores the multilingual skull-and-crossbones warning signs to test either the temperature of a two-hundred-degree acidic pool or the patience of a two-thousand-pound bull bison.

That lack of softness deeply offended @FlatFluffer, who wrote "Uncushioned Chaos!" to complain that "Pillowstone National Park" was "just a lumpy, unsupportive bed accessory" in constant need of fluffing. Packing your pajamas and driving into the Teton wilderness expecting a volcanic caldera to feel like a goose-down mattress from a department store white sale is a bold misinterpretation of the National Park Service mission. The only thing getting fluffed in the Hayden Valley is the winter coat of a grizzly bear, and attempting to rest your head on either the wildlife or the travertine limestone terraces at Mammoth Hot Springs will end in an airlift to Idaho Falls.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Stay strictly on the wooden boardwalks in all thermal areas, carry EPA-approved bear spray on your hip while hiking, and keep at least twenty-five yards away from every bison.
